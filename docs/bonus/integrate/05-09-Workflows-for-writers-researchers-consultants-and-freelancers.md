# Chapter 5.9: Workflows for writers, researchers, consultants, and freelancers

If you work independently, you're the researcher, the writer, the project manager, and the accounts department. Copilot can take on some of each: finding and organizing sources, producing first drafts, and tidying a proposal at the end of a long day. You also carry responsibilities that an employee can leave to their organization. Your clients' information is in your hands, on your account. Your name is on the work. And nobody else has written the rules for how you use AI, so you have to write them yourself. In this chapter, you'll see three workflows for researching and organizing source material, drafting briefs, reports, and proposals, and protecting client data, intellectual property, and authorship. By the end, you'll have a client project set up with its own Notebook, and a short statement of how you use AI in your work.

> **Plan note**: This chapter assumes a personal account with Microsoft 365 Premium, which includes Researcher, Notebooks, and Cowork. Most of the workflows also work with Personal or Family, using Chat's web search in place of Researcher. A personal account doesn't have the data protections of a work account, which affects what you should put into it. If a client gives you an account in their organization, use that account for their work.

## Where Copilot fits in independent work

| Copilot helps with | Keep with yourself |
| --- | --- |
| Finding and organizing sources | Judging which sources to trust |
| Summarizing long material | The argument, the insight, and the conclusions |
| First drafts of structure and routine sections | Your voice |
| Checking a draft against a brief | The advice you give a client |
| Proposals, scopes, and routine correspondence | Your price, your terms, and what you promise |

For independent professionals, the right-hand column is the product. Clients pay for your judgment and your voice. Use Copilot on the work around them.

## Workflow 1: Research and organize source material

Research for a client project produces a pile of material: reports, articles, interview notes, the client's own documents. A Notebook keeps it together and lets you question it.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **When** | At the start of a project, and as material arrives |
| **Inputs** | Public sources, and client documents that you're permitted to use with AI |
| **Tool** | A Notebook for each client or project (Chapter 1.11). Researcher or Chat for public sources (Chapters 1.8 and 1.9) |
| **Check** | Every source opened. Every claim you'll use traced to a primary source. A source log kept (Chapter 1.12) |
| **Stays with you** | Which sources to trust, and what they add up to |

Set up each project the same way:

1. **One Notebook for each client.** Never mix two clients' material. Copilot answers from everything in a Notebook, and a detail from one client could appear in work for another.
2. **Notebook instructions that state the rules.** For example: "Use only the references in this notebook. Give the reference for every point. Say when the references don't cover something."
3. **A source log from the first day**, with what you checked and when.

For public research, use the source-based questions from Chapter 1.8 and the research brief from Chapter 1.9. Then ask the Notebook questions that organize what you have:

- `Group the sources by the position they take on [question]. Which sources disagree, and about what?`
- `What do these sources not cover that a reader would expect?`
- `List every statistic in these sources, with its original source and date.`

For interviews and your own notes, the cautions from Chapter 2.7 apply. Copilot turns "she seemed doubtful about the timeline" into "she rejected the timeline." Check each summary against your notes, and keep the notes.

Researchers and writers have a particular duty over quotations and citations. Never use a quotation that you haven't found in its original source, word for word. Never cite a work that you haven't opened. Copilot can invent both.

## Workflow 2: Draft briefs, reports, and proposals

Independent work involves a lot of writing around the writing: proposals, scopes of work, progress reports, and summaries. These follow patterns, which makes them good candidates for saved prompts.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **When** | Winning work, starting a project, and reporting on it |
| **Inputs** | The client's request, your notes from conversations, your own past documents as examples, and your source log |
| **Tool** | Copilot Chat or Word (Chapter 2.3). Saved prompts for documents you write often (Chapter 4.2) |
| **Check** | Every fact against your source log. Every commitment, date, and price. The draft against the client's brief |
| **Stays with you** | The argument, the recommendations, your voice, and your terms |

**For proposals**, give Copilot the client's words and your own structure:

```
Draft a proposal in response to the attached request from [client]. Use the structure of my attached past proposal. Include: my understanding of what they need, in their own words where possible; the approach, in four or five steps; what I'll deliver; what I need from them; and the timeline. Leave the price and terms as [TO ADD]. Don't promise outcomes or results. Mark anything in their request that's unclear with [ASK CLIENT].
```

Leaving the price and terms for yourself is on purpose. They're your decisions, and a figure that Copilot invents may end up in a document you send.

The **[ASK CLIENT]** markers are often the most valuable part. An unclear requirement that's found at the proposal stage costs an email. Found at delivery, it costs a rewrite.

**For reports**, separate the structure from the substance. Ask Copilot to check your outline against the brief, and to draft the routine sections, such as the method and the summary of sources. Write the findings and the recommendations yourself. Then ask Copilot to review:

```
Compare my draft with the client's brief. Which questions in the brief does the draft answer fully, partly, or not at all? Where does the draft make a claim without a source from my source log?
```

**For your own voice**, give Copilot examples of your writing and ask it to follow their sentence length and tone. Then rewrite the opening and the conclusion yourself. A reader forms a view of who wrote a document from its first and last paragraphs.

> **Watch out**: A proposal or scope written with Copilot can commit you to more than you intended. Drafts tend to add reassuring phrases, such as "comprehensive analysis," "all relevant stakeholders," or "ongoing support." Each one is something a client could hold you to. Read every deliverable and every adjective in a proposal as a promise, and remove the ones you wouldn't want to be measured against.

## Workflow 3: Protect client data, intellectual property, and authorship

An employee's organization sets the rules for AI. As an independent professional, you set them, and your clients are relying on you to set them well.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **When** | Before you take on a client, and before you put anything of theirs into Copilot |
| **Inputs** | Your contract, any confidentiality agreement, and the client's own AI policy |
| **Tool** | Your judgment. Copilot can help you draft your policy |
| **Check** | What each contract permits |
| **Stays with you** | All of it |

**Client data.** Before you upload a client's document, ask three questions:

- **Does my contract allow it?** Some contracts and confidentiality agreements forbid sharing the client's information with third parties, or with AI services specifically. If the contract is silent, ask the client.
- **Does it need to go in?** Often you can work from a description, or remove names and figures first.
- **Is this the right account?** A personal account doesn't carry enterprise protections. For sensitive work, ask whether the client can give you an account in their organization.

Never put into a personal account: personal data about the client's customers or staff, unpublished financial results, legal matters, or anything the client has marked confidential.

**Intellectual property.** Three issues come up:

- **What you give Copilot.** Don't upload material that you don't have the right to use, such as a paid report or a competitor's confidential document.
- **What Copilot gives you.** Generated text can resemble existing published work. For anything you'll publish, check distinctive phrases, and never ask Copilot to write in the style of a named living author.
- **Who owns the result.** The ownership of AI-assisted work is treated differently in different countries, and the law is still developing. If ownership of the deliverable is important to your client, say how you work, and take legal advice on your contract terms.

**Authorship.** Your name on a piece of work is a statement that you stand behind it. That holds whether or not Copilot helped. Three practices keep it true:

- **Disclose how you work.** Tell clients, in your proposal or your terms, how you use AI: for research support and drafting, with every source checked and every conclusion your own. Some clients, publishers, and journals require disclosure, and some forbid AI use for certain work. Ask.
- **Do the thinking.** If Copilot wrote the argument, it isn't yours, and you may not be able to defend it when the client asks a question.
- **Charge fairly.** If you bill by the hour, bill for the hours you worked. Pricing by the result, instead of by the hour, avoids this question.

> **Good practice**: Write a half-page AI policy for your own practice, and attach it to your proposals. Say what you use AI for, what you never put into it, how you check its output, and that you take responsibility for everything you deliver. It answers a question that a client may hesitate to ask.

## Keep your business information in order

The same tools help with running your practice. A weekly prompt can list invoices to send, proposals awaiting a reply, and deadlines in the next two weeks, drawn from your own calendar and files. Use the personal work system from Chapter 5.1.

Keep your own business records, such as contracts, invoices, and tax documents, apart from client project Notebooks. For tax and legal questions, Copilot can help you prepare questions for an accountant or a lawyer. It doesn't replace one.

## Try it now

Set up one client project safely, and write your own AI policy:

1. Choose a current or recent project. Read the contract and any confidentiality agreement for what they say about sharing information and about AI. If they say nothing, note that you need to ask.
2. Create a Notebook for that client alone. Add only material that you're permitted to use.
3. Add Notebook instructions that tell Copilot to use only the references and to give the reference for each point.
4. Start a source log for the project.
5. Ask the Notebook to group the sources by the position they take, and to list what they don't cover.
6. Take a proposal or report that you need to write. Use the proposal prompt from this chapter, or ask Copilot to check your draft against the client's brief.
7. Find every deliverable and every adjective in the draft that reads as a promise. Remove any that you wouldn't want to be measured against.
8. Write a half-page AI policy for your practice. Ask Copilot to review it:

   ```
   Here is my policy on how I use AI in client work: [paste it]. What would a cautious client still want to know? What have I left unclear?
   ```

You should now have a Notebook for one client with only permitted material, a source log, a draft checked against its brief, and a half-page AI policy.

> **What to ask next**: Ask Copilot to prepare you for a client's questions: `A client asks, "Did you use AI to write this report?" Based on my policy, draft an honest answer in three sentences. Then list two follow-up questions they might ask.`

### Check the result

- [ ] Did you check what your contract permits before uploading client material?
- [ ] Does the Notebook contain material from one client only?
- [ ] Have you opened every source you plan to cite, and found every quotation in its original?
- [ ] Does your AI policy say what you never put into Copilot, and who is responsible for what you deliver?

> **Real-world example**: A freelance policy researcher is commissioned by a charity to write a report on rural transport. Her contract doesn't mention AI, so she emails the charity to ask. They agree to her using it for public research, and ask her not to upload their donor survey, which contains personal details. She builds a Notebook from public reports only, keeps a source log, and works on the survey in Excel on her own computer. Her report includes a short note on method that says how AI was used and that she checked every source.
