# Chapter 5.12: Complete a 30-day Copilot adoption plan

Reading about a method and using it are different. This last chapter is a plan for the next 30 days, in which you take one recurring problem from your own work and turn it into a routine that you've tested, made safe, and measured. It uses what you've already learned and adds nothing new. Each week has one job, a small amount of time each day, and something to show at the end. In this chapter, you'll see the four weeks laid out, with what to do and what to check in each. By the end of the 30 days, you'll have one working routine with evidence behind it, and a second one chosen.

> **Sample files**: This chapter uses `adoption-plan.docx` from the `Book5/Chapter5.12` folder. It's a four-week planner with space for each week's tasks, results, and notes. You'll also use `value-tracker.xlsx` from Chapter 5.11 and `workflow-canvas.docx` from Chapter 4.12.

Allow 15 to 20 minutes on most working days, plus the time the task itself takes. Put that time in your calendar now. A plan with no time attached to it doesn't get done.

One task is enough. The purpose of the 30 days is to complete the whole cycle once, so that you know how each stage goes. Three half-finished routines teach you less than one that's finished.

## Week 1: Choose one recurring, low-risk problem

The first week is about choosing well and recording where you're starting from. You won't change how you do the task yet.

Follow these steps in week 1:

1. Open your inventory and your short list from Chapter 5.1. If you haven't made them, do that first.
2. Choose one task, using the four tests from that chapter: frequent, low-risk, yours, and annoying. Weekly or daily is best, so that you get several runs within the month.
3. Score it against the questions in Chapter 4.1. If a mistake would be serious and hard to spot, or if the task involves a decision about a person, choose a different one.
4. Write the purpose of the routine in one sentence: what it does, for whom, how often, and who checks it.
5. Do the task by hand, as you normally would, and time it. Record the time, any rework, any errors, and how the recipient found the result, in the baseline sheet of `value-tracker.xlsx`.
6. Repeat the measurement each time the task comes up this week.
7. Write your test for success: the result that would make you keep the routine, and the result that would make you stop.
8. Tell one person what you're doing, such as a colleague or your manager. You're more likely to finish something that someone else knows about.

**At the end of week 1**, you should have a task with a one-sentence purpose, at least one baseline measurement, and a written test for success.

> **Watch out**: A common way for this plan to fail is choosing a task that's too large. "Automate my monthly reporting" is a project. "Draft the first section of the monthly report from the sales workbook" is a task. If you can't do it by hand in under an hour, choose a smaller piece of it.

## Week 2: Test prompts and document the manual process

In the second week, you learn how Copilot handles the task, and you write down what the task involves. You're still running everything by hand.

Follow these steps in week 2:

1. Write down the manual process as a numbered list: where the information comes from, what you do with it, what you check, and where the result goes. Include the judgment calls, such as "I leave out anything the client hasn't confirmed."
2. Mark each step that only a person should do. Those stay with you.
3. Write a brief for Copilot, using the six parts from Chapter 1.5. Put your judgment calls into the constraints and the quality bar.
4. Run it, and compare the result with what you'd have produced by hand.
5. Improve it with the follow-up techniques from Chapter 1.6. Ask what Copilot assumed. Fix one problem at a time.
6. Fold your corrections into a single prompt, as in Chapter 4.2. Mark the parameters.
7. Test the prompt in a new chat on each occasion that the task comes up this week. Aim for three runs that need no follow-up.
8. Add at least one line to the quality bar that makes a failure visible, such as a row count or a statement of what's missing.
9. Save the prompt with a clear name and today's date.

**At the end of week 2**, you should have a written process with the human steps marked, and a saved prompt that produces a usable result three times in a row.

If the prompt doesn't reach that standard by the end of the week, that's a result too. The task may need better sources, or it may be one that Copilot doesn't do well. Return to your short list, and choose another. You've lost a week and learned something that's worth knowing.

Writing down the manual process often turns out to be useful by itself. You may find steps that you've been doing from habit and that nobody needs.

## Week 3: Create a repeatable workflow with review gates

In the third week, you decide how far up the automation ladder this task should go, and you build the checks around it.

Follow these steps in week 3:

1. Decide the rung, using the ladder from Chapter 4.1. For many first routines, a saved prompt that you run yourself at a set time is the right answer. Move to a scheduled draft only if the prompt has stopped changing.
2. Open `workflow-canvas.docx`, and fill in all eight boxes for the routine: trigger, inputs, actions, permissions, approval, deliverable, failure handling, and quality check.
3. Look at box 5, the approval. Make sure a person reads the result before it reaches anyone else. If the output goes only to you, your own review is the gate.
4. Pass the routine through the four gates from Chapter 5.10: privacy, rights, accuracy, and consequences. Change the prompt or the inputs if a gate finds a problem.
5. If you're scheduling it, replace the parameters, test what happens when a source is missing, and set it up as a draft, as in Chapter 4.3.
6. Run the routine as designed on each occasion this week. Time each part, and record the results on the trial sheet of `value-tracker.xlsx`.
7. Test one failure on purpose. Remove or rename an input, and check that the routine stops and tells you.
8. Add the routine to your register of automations from Chapter 4.12, with its owner and a review date.
9. Attach the routine to a moment in your week, as Chapter 5.1 suggested.

**At the end of week 3**, you should have a completed canvas, a routine running on the rung you chose, at least one tested failure, and the first trial measurements.

Don't climb higher than you need to. A saved prompt, run every Monday at 9 AM because it's in your calendar, is a complete and respectable result for this plan.

## Week 4: Measure results, refine, and select the next opportunity

In the final week, you find out whether the routine is worth keeping, improve it, and decide what comes next.

Follow these steps in week 4:

1. Keep running the routine, and keep recording time, rework, errors, and cost.
2. Ask the recipient the one question from Chapter 5.11: is this more useful, less useful, or about the same as before?
3. At the end of the week, compare the trial with the baseline on the summary sheet.
4. Apply the test that you wrote in week 1. Decide: keep, improve, or stop.
5. If you're keeping or improving it, make one refinement. Choose the correction you made most often during review, and build it into the prompt.
6. Decide what the saved time is for, and put it in your calendar.
7. Update your one-page system from Chapter 5.1 with the routine, its result, and the date you'll measure it again.
8. Write three lines on what you learned: what worked, what took longer than you expected, and what you'd do differently.
9. Choose your next task from the short list. Use what you learned to choose a better one.

**At the end of week 4**, you should have a comparison with the baseline, a decision with a reason, one refinement made, and your next task chosen.

If the decision is to stop, the plan has still worked. You've learned how to tell a routine that pays its way from one that doesn't, and you've avoided keeping one that doesn't. Start again with the next task on your list, which will go faster.

> **Good practice**: Share your three lines with the person you told in week 1. Explaining what you learned makes it stick, and your colleague may have a task that the same routine would fit.

## Keep the system working after 30 days

One routine is the beginning of a system. Three habits keep it alive.

**Add slowly.** Start one new routine at a time, each through the same four weeks. After a few rounds, the stages will go faster, and you may run two at once. A system of five or six routines that you trust is a good place to settle.

**Review monthly.** Put 30 minutes in your calendar each month to look at your one-page system and your register:

- Is each routine still used?
- Has anything it depends on changed, such as a file, a folder, or a person?
- When was each one last measured?
- Is there one to switch off?

**Expect Copilot to change.** The menus, the plans, and the features described in this book will move. Microsoft has reorganized the Copilot app more than once, and it may again. What you've built doesn't depend on any one screen. A clear brief, a checked source, a review before sending, and a measured result work with whatever the app looks like next year. When a feature changes, check the book's online companion page, retest the routines that used it, and carry on.

## Try it now

Start the 30-day plan today:

1. Open `adoption-plan.docx`, and write today's date at the top. Write the date 30 days from now beside it.
2. Put 15 to 20 minutes in your calendar on each working day for the next four weeks. Label each week with its job: choose, test, build, and measure.
3. Open your short list from Chapter 5.1, and choose your task with the four tests.
4. Write its purpose in one sentence.
5. Write your test for success.
6. Do the task by hand at the next opportunity, time it, and record your first baseline measurement.
7. Tell one person what you're doing, and when you'll tell them the result.

You should now have a dated plan, time set aside in your calendar, a chosen task with a purpose and a test for success, and your first measurement.

> **What to ask next**: Ask Copilot to check your choice before you commit a month to it: `For the next 30 days, I plan to turn this task into a Copilot routine: [your one-sentence purpose]. What could make this a poor choice for a first routine? What should I find out in the first week?`

### Check the result

- [ ] Is the task small enough to do by hand in under an hour?
- [ ] Is time for the plan in your calendar for all four weeks?
- [ ] Have you written what result would make you keep the routine, and what would make you stop?
- [ ] Does someone else know that you've started?

> **Real-world example**: A scheduler at a home care agency chooses one task for her 30 days: the Friday summary of next week's unfilled visits, which takes her 50 minutes. In week 1, she times it three times. In week 2, her prompt keeps missing visits that were canceled and rebooked, until she adds a line that tells Copilot to use the latest status of each visit. In week 3, she runs it as a saved prompt every Friday at 2 PM, and she reads every line before sending it to the care managers. In week 4, her figures show 50 minutes reduced to 20, with one error caught in her review and none reaching the managers. She keeps it, and chooses the monthly mileage report as her next task.

## What you've built

You began this book by asking Copilot a single focused question and checking the answer. Since then, you've given it your own files, searched the web and tested the sources, written and verified documents, analyzed data, and built presentations. You've worked with colleagues' messages and meetings without misreporting them, and with shared files without widening who can see them. You've automated a task and placed a person at each point where it could do harm. And you've fitted these into your own work, with checks that protect the people who rely on you and measurements that show what's worth keeping.

That's more than a tour of features. Features change, and some of the ones described here will look different by the time you read this. What you've built is a way of working: brief clearly, check the result, keep a person responsible, and measure whether it helped. That will serve you with the next version of Copilot, and with whatever tool comes after it.

The 30-day plan gives you one routine. Run it again for the next task, and the one after that. Keep the routines that earn their place, and switch off the ones that don't. For changes to plans, menus, and preview features after this book went to print, see the online companion page. Throughout, the rule has stayed the same: Copilot proposes, and you decide.
