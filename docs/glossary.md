# Everything AI Visual Bible glossary

This glossary explains about 60 of the terms used most often in *Everything AI Visual Bible*, from the Introduction through Book 6. Each term has one paragraph, and the terms are in alphabetical order. The short glossary in the Introduction gives quick definitions. This version adds the reasons behind each term and points you to the place in the volume where you use it.

## A

### Accessibility

Accessibility means making things usable by people with disabilities, whether the disability affects sight, hearing, movement, speech, or understanding. AI helps in several ways: it can describe photos aloud, turn speech into text, read text in a natural voice, and simplify complicated writing. Screen readers such as VoiceOver on Apple devices and TalkBack on Android now use AI to describe pictures and buttons that have no text label. These tools make mistakes, so check any description that you depend on. Chapter 5.10 covers accessibility in detail.

### Agent

An agent is an AI that can take actions for you, such as browsing websites, filling in forms, sending messages, or making purchases, usually after you give it permission. An ordinary assistant answers a question and stops. An agent works through several steps toward a goal and may act in your accounts while you are away. Because an agent can change things outside the chat, give it a small first task, limit what it can access, and approve actions that send, buy, publish, or delete. Chapter 4.8 introduces agents, and Book 6 explains how to use them safely.

### AI (artificial intelligence)

AI is the name for computer systems that do tasks which usually need human thinking, such as understanding language, recognizing pictures, or making recommendations. The term covers many methods, from the spam filter in your email to the assistants in this volume. AI doesn't think or understand the way a person does. It finds patterns in large amounts of data and uses them to produce a result. Chapter 1.1 explains what AI is and where you already use it.

### AI companion

An AI companion is a chatbot designed to act as a friend, a partner, or a character, with a name, a personality, and often a memory of past conversations. Some people find companions comforting, especially when they feel lonely. Companion apps are designed to keep people talking, and they can collect very personal information. Parents should know which companion apps their children use and talk with them about how the apps work. Chapter 5.4 covers companions in the context of family life.

### Alt text

Alt text is a short written description of a picture, read aloud by a screen reader for people who can't see the image. Good alt text describes the parts of the picture that matter in context, not every detail. An assistant can draft alt text for a photo, a chart, or a slide in seconds. Read each draft and correct it, because the assistant may miss what the picture is meant to show. Chapter 5.10 and the chapters on slides and websites use alt text.

### Ambient AI

Ambient AI is built into the devices and services you already use, with access to your own messages, files, and calendar. It can help without you opening a separate app. Siri AI on Apple devices, Gemini Intelligence on Android, and Copilot inside Microsoft 365 are the examples this volume uses. Ambient AI is convenient because it already knows your context, and for the same reason you need to check what it can see. The Introduction compares ambient AI with destination AI.

### Assistant

An assistant is an app you talk to in everyday language, such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or Copilot. People also call it a chatbot. This volume uses **assistant** for the product you use and **model** for the trained system inside it. One assistant can offer several models, and the same model can appear in several products. The distinction helps when a chapter says that a feature belongs to the assistant, such as web search or memory, and not to the model.

### Automation

Automation means setting up a task so that software does it for you whenever a condition is met, without you starting it each time. A simple automation might save every email attachment to a folder or send you a summary each Friday. No-code tools let you build one by choosing a trigger and an action. Adding an AI step lets the automation read, sort, or summarize text along the way. Chapter 4.7 shows you how to build one without writing code.

## B

### Base model

A base model is the result of pre-training: a language model that has learned to continue text but hasn't yet learned to be a helpful assistant. If you type a question to a base model, it may continue with more questions, because web pages that contain one question often contain others. Companies turn a base model into an assistant with further training, including fine-tuning and human feedback. Chapter 1.7 shows the whole pipeline in a diagram.

### Bias

In AI, bias means a pattern in a system's answers that unfairly favors or disadvantages some people or ideas. Bias usually comes from the training data, which reflects the unfair patterns in the text and pictures that people have produced. It can appear as a stereotype in a story, as an assumption in advice, or as an unfair result when AI helps make a decision about a person. You can reduce bias by asking for several perspectives and by checking what assumptions the answer makes. Chapter 1.8 introduces bias, and Book 6 explains how to respond to it.

### Brief

A brief is a short written description of a project that you give an assistant, covering the goal, the audience, the tone, the length, and anything to avoid. Writing a prompt is like briefing a capable new colleague on their first day: they know a lot in general, but nothing about you or your work. In Chapter 3.10, one brief feeds every tool in a creative project, so the text, images, and slides stay consistent with each other.

## C

### Citation

A citation is a reference that tells you where a fact came from, such as a link, an article title, or a document name. Assistants that search the web or read your files can add citations to their answers. A citation helps only when you open it and confirm that the source says what the assistant claims. Assistants also invent citations that look perfect, with a real journal, a real author, and a believable title. Chapter 4.1 shows you how to check and cite sources.

### Connector

A connector is a link you set up between an assistant and another account, such as your email, calendar, or cloud storage, so the assistant can read from it. Once connected, the assistant can answer questions about your own messages and files. A connector gives the assistant access to private information, so connect only what a task needs and review the permissions you grant. Book 6 explains connections and permissions.

### Content credentials

Content credentials are hidden information attached to a file that records how it was made, including whether AI was used. An industry group called the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) created the standard. A checker tool can show the credentials on a picture or video. Missing credentials don't prove that a picture is fake, because most pictures don't have them yet. Chapter 3.8 introduces content credentials, and Book 6 uses them to check images.

### Context

Context is everything a model uses to write its next reply: your message, the earlier messages in the chat, files you added, saved notes such as custom instructions, and results from a web search. The model knows nothing about you or your task beyond its context, so a prompt with good context gets a better answer. Giving context is the most reliable way to improve a result. Chapter 2.3 shows you how to give files, examples, and background.

### Context window

The context window is the limit on how much context a model can consider at once. The book compares it to a desk: everything the model works with must fit on the desk, and when the desk is full, something has to come off. This is why a long chat can lose track of details from the beginning. When you see signs of this, start a new chat and bring in only what you need. Chapter 1.5 explains the context window, and Chapter 2.5 lists the warning signs.

### Custom instructions

Custom instructions are information you give an assistant once, such as your job, your preferred writing style, or the format you like, so that it uses the information in every chat. They are a form of saved context placed on the desk at the start of each conversation. Keep them short and general, because anything you put there applies to every chat, including ones where it doesn't belong. Chapter 2.7 shows you how to write them.

## D

### Data residency

Data residency is the country or region where a company stores and processes your messages and files. It matters because the laws of that place may decide who can access your data. Check a company's help center for a statement about where it stores data, because assistants are often wrong about their own settings and features. Book 6 explains data residency together with jurisdiction and sovereign AI.

### Deep research

Deep research is a mode in which an assistant works on a question for several minutes, running many searches, reading the pages it finds, and writing a long report with sources. It suits questions where a single search would give a thin answer. The report can still contain errors, so open the sources it lists and check the claims that matter. Chapter 4.1 explains when deep research is worth the wait.

### Deepfake

A deepfake is a fake image, video, or voice recording made with AI to look or sound like a real person saying or doing something they didn't say or do. Convincing voice clones can now be made from a short sample, such as a video posted on social media. Because looking for visual flaws is increasingly unreliable, Book 6 teaches habits that work regardless of quality: pause, check through a channel you already trust, call back, and agree a family code word.

### Destination AI

A destination AI is an assistant you open on purpose, in its own app or browser tab, and give your question or document to. ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity are the main examples. A destination AI has no standing access to your personal accounts unless you connect them. Copilot and Gemini act as destination AI when you use their own apps and websites, and as ambient AI when they are built into other products.

### Diffusion

Diffusion is a method many image generators use to make a picture. During training, the model learns to clean up noise, step by step, until a clear image appears. To make a new picture, it starts from random noise and removes the noise a little at a time, guided by the words in your prompt. This is why each detail in a prompt guides another part of the result, and why the same prompt can give different pictures. Chapter 1.6 explains the idea, and Chapters 3.3 and 3.4 use it.

### Disclosure

Disclosure means telling people that AI played a part in something you made or did. Disclosing builds trust, because people judge work differently depending on how it was made, and it protects you when a school, employer, or platform has rules about AI. In general, work in which AI created the content calls for disclosure, while using AI to check spelling or brainstorm usually does not. Some places now require labels by law. Book 6 includes a guide to disclosure by situation.

## F

### Fact sheet

A fact sheet is a single document that holds every fact a project uses, such as dates, prices, names, addresses, phone numbers, and rules. Pasting it into every prompt means each tool works from the same facts and you don't have to correct the same mistake repeatedly. It also gives you one place to check before you publish. Chapter 3.10 pairs the fact sheet with a brief and a style card.

### Few-shot prompting

Few-shot prompting means giving the assistant several examples (the "shots") so that it continues a pattern, such as a way of sorting, labeling, or phrasing items. One example sets a style. Several examples teach a pattern. Include examples that cover the range of cases you expect, and check that the assistant follows the pattern consistently. Chapter 2.6 covers few-shot prompting among the advanced prompt patterns.

### Fine-tuning

Fine-tuning is a training stage after pre-training in which a model learns from examples of good conversations written or chosen by people. It learns to answer questions, follow instructions, and use a helpful tone. Fine-tuning is part of how a base model becomes an assistant. Chapter 1.7 places fine-tuning in the sequence of stages that follow pre-training.

### Frontier model

A frontier model is one of the largest and most capable AI models, built to push what AI can do. Only a small number of companies make them, and they usually run in the company's data centers, not on a phone. Most frontier models are closed, so you can use them only through the company's own apps and services. Chapter 1.9 maps who makes which models.

## G

### Generative AI

Generative AI creates new content, such as text, images, audio, or video, from your description. It is one kind of deep learning, which is one kind of machine learning, which is one way to build AI. Because a generative model produces new content each time, it can be creative and it can be wrong. You can treat what it produces as a draft that needs your review. Book 3 is about creating with generative AI.

## H

### Hallucination

A hallucination is a confident-sounding answer from an AI that is wrong or invented. The name is borrowed from psychology, but nothing like a human hallucination is happening. The model is writing statistically likely text, and likely text is not always true. Hallucinations are especially common in references and citations, quotations, exact numbers and dates, details about small businesses or little-known people, and descriptions of the AI's own features. Chapter 1.8 shows you how to spot them.

### Human feedback (RLHF)

Human feedback is a training stage in which people compare several answers to the same question and pick the better one. The model is then trained to produce more answers like the preferred ones. Experts call this reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Human feedback shapes an assistant's personality as much as its knowledge, which is why one assistant sounds formal and another chatty. It can also teach a model to tell people what they want to hear, a problem called sycophancy.

## I

### Indemnity

An indemnity is a promise by an AI company to defend you if someone claims that your use of its output infringes their copyright, within stated limits. Some business plans include one and personal plans usually do not. If you plan to use AI-made work commercially, check the company's terms for an indemnity, and read the conditions. Book 6 covers copyright, ownership, and licensing.

## J

### Jurisdiction

A jurisdiction is the area where a set of laws applies, such as a country, a state, or a trading bloc such as the European Union. When you send a message to an assistant, it often travels to a data center in another place, and the laws of that place may affect who can access it. Jurisdiction and data residency together tell you which rules protect your data. Book 6 helps you decide which kind of assistant suits which kind of task.

## K

### Knowledge base

A knowledge base is a collection of your own documents and notes that an AI tool can search and answer questions about. It can hold far more than fits in the context window, so the tool retrieves only the passages most likely to contain the answer. Good tools show citations so you can check each answer against the original. Chapter 4.5 shows you how to build a small knowledge base and ask questions across it.

## L

### Large language model (LLM)

A large language model is the kind of AI model behind most assistants. It learned patterns from huge amounts of text and writes by predicting the next token, one after another. Predicting the next word sounds like a small skill, but doing it well across billions of pages requires picking up grammar, facts, and common reasoning steps. An LLM is good at making answers plausible and has no equivalent skill for making them true, which is why checking matters. Chapter 1.4 explains how LLMs work.

### Lateral reading

Lateral reading is a checking method in which you leave the answer you are reading, open a new browser tab, and find out what other sources say about it. Fact-checkers use it because it is faster and more reliable than studying a single page closely. Use it on AI answers by searching for the claim, looking for an independent source, and going to a primary source where you can. Book 6 builds its routine for checking answers around this habit.

## M

### Machine learning

Machine learning is a way of building AI by showing a computer many examples instead of writing rules by hand. The learning process is called training, and the result is a model: a file of learned patterns that a program uses to decide what to do with new examples it hasn't seen. Image classifiers, spam filters, and language models are all built this way. Chapter 1.2 explains machine learning and has you train a small model of your own.

### Markdown

Markdown is a simple way of marking formatting in plain text, using symbols such as `#` for a heading, `**` for bold, and `-` for a list item. Assistants often write in Markdown, which is why a reply can show stray symbols when it is pasted somewhere that doesn't convert them. You can ask an assistant for plain text instead, or for a table, a list, or a heading structure. Chapter 2.4 covers formats.

### Memory

Memory is an assistant feature that saves facts about you between chats, such as your job, your family, or your preferences. You can usually view and delete what it saves, and you can start a temporary or incognito chat that is excluded from memory. Memory is useful for facts that stay true and unhelpful for facts that change. Review it from time to time. Chapter 2.7 shows you how to view and control it.

### Model

A model is the trained AI system that produces answers. One assistant can offer several models, such as a fast one for quick questions and a more capable one for harder work. Models differ in knowledge, speed, cost, and skills. This volume avoids relying on one model, because models are replaced often, while the skills for using them last. Chapter 1.9 compares the companies and tools.

### Multimodal

A multimodal model can work with more than one kind of input, such as text, photos, and voice, in one conversation. Early AI tools needed a separate model for each kind of input. A multimodal assistant lets you photograph a problem, speak a question, or upload a screenshot and ask about it. Chapter 1.6 explains how the technology works, and Chapter 2.8 shows you how to use voice and camera.

## N

### Neural network

A neural network is a structure of many simple connected units that adjust to produce the right outputs for given inputs. It is loosely inspired by the brain, although it works in a very different way. Each connection has a weight, and training adjusts the weights until the network gets the examples right. Language models, image generators, and speech tools are all neural networks. Chapter 1.3 explains neural networks without the math.

## O

### On-device model

An on-device model runs on your phone or computer, so a request it handles doesn't leave your device. It can work without an internet connection and keeps more of your information private. On-device models are smaller than frontier models, so they handle simpler tasks, and your device may send harder requests to a larger model in the cloud. Chapter 5.8 looks at the AI already on your phone and computer.

### Open-weight model

An open-weight model is a model whose weights the company has published, so anyone can download it and run it on their own computer. Examples include Meta's Llama, Google's Gemma, and Mistral's models. Open-weight models are not the same as fully open source, because the training data and training code may not be shared. They give you more control over where your data goes, and they usually need more technical skill to set up. Chapter 1.9 introduces them.

## P

### Parameters (weights)

Parameters are the adjustable numbers inside a neural network. Each one is a weight, which acts like a dial: a high weight means "pay a lot of attention to this signal," and a low weight means "mostly ignore it." The total number of weights is the model's parameter count, which news stories use to describe a model's size. More parameters don't always mean a better model. Chapter 1.3 explains weights and parameters.

### Personal context

Personal context is an AI's ability to search your own messages, email, photos, and files to answer a question, after you allow it. Apple and Google each use their own name for the feature. It lets you ask things such as when a flight lands, using the confirmation email you received. Because the feature reads private information, review what you have allowed it to see. Chapter 5.8 covers it.

### Plugin and skill

A plugin is an add-on that gives an assistant new abilities or links it to other services. A skill is a saved set of instructions, and sometimes files, that teaches an assistant to do a particular task the same way each time. Skills can hold detailed, multi-step workflows. Some providers are replacing custom GPTs and Gems with skills, and availability depends on your plan, so check the provider's help center. Chapters 2.7 and 4.8 introduce them, and Book 6 explains how to use plugins safely.

### Pre-training

Pre-training is the first stage of building a language model, in which the model reads an enormous amount of text and learns to predict the next token. The text typically includes public web pages, books, articles, computer code, and licensed material. Pre-training takes weeks or months on thousands of specialized chips, and for the largest models it costs many millions of dollars. The result is a base model. Chapter 1.7 describes pre-training and the questions about who owns the training material.

### Primary source

A primary source is the original: the government's own statistics, the text of a law, the original study, or a company's own announcement. A secondary source, such as a news report or a blog, describes the original and can misread it. When an AI answer matters, find the primary source and check that it says what the assistant claims. Chapters 4.1 and Book 6 use primary sources as the final check.

### Project

A project is a workspace where you collect files, instructions, and chats for one area of work or life. Every chat you start inside a project begins with the project's files and instructions already in the context. Assistants use different names for the feature, including projects, spaces, notebooks, and gems. A project saves you from pasting the same background into each chat. Chapter 2.7 shows you how to set one up.

### Prompt

A prompt is the message you send an assistant: what you want it to do and what a good answer should include. Writing a good prompt is like briefing a capable new colleague. You don't need special words or secret formulas. You need a clear role, task, context, format, and constraints. The same prompt can give different answers each time, so treat the result as a first draft. Chapter 2.2 teaches the five-part recipe.

### Prompt injection

Prompt injection is a hidden instruction, for example inside a web page, an email, or a document, that tries to make an AI do something you didn't ask for. An assistant that reads untrusted content can mistake the instruction for part of your request. The risk is greatest for agents, which can act on what they read. Limit what an agent can access, don't let it act on untrusted content without your approval, and review actions before you approve them. Book 6 covers it.

### Prompt library

A prompt library is your own collection of saved prompts that have worked well, each with a short searchable name, a note on what it does, and the places where you change it. It saves you from searching old chats and rewriting prompts from memory. Store it somewhere you can reach easily, such as a document or a notes app, and update a prompt when you find a better version. Chapter 2.10 shows you how to start one.

## R

### Reasoning model

A reasoning model works through a problem before it answers. It writes out steps first, such as breaking a question into parts or checking a calculation, and then writes the final answer. Companies also call these thinking models. Some assistants show the working and some hide it. Reasoning models are slower and often better at math, logic, and multi-step problems. Their final answers can still be wrong, so check the results that matter. Chapter 1.4 introduces them.

### Retrieval

Retrieval is the step in which a tool searches your documents for the passages most likely to contain the answer, places them in the context window, and writes an answer from them. It lets an assistant answer from far more material than fits in a single chat. Retrieval is how knowledge bases and many web-search features work. Chapter 4.5 includes a diagram of the steps.

### Reverse image search

A reverse image search takes a picture and finds where else it has appeared online, such as with Google Lens or TinEye. It can show where a picture first appeared and whether someone has changed it. Use it before you trust a suspicious photo, and before you publish an AI picture that might closely resemble an existing work. It doesn't work on every image. Book 6 uses it for checking images and for copyright.

## S

### Skill

See [Plugin and skill](#plugin-and-skill).

### Sovereign AI

Sovereign AI describes a country, region, or organization that can build, run, and control AI with its own computers, data, and rules, without depending on companies elsewhere. Governments use the term when they plan new data centers or choose AI suppliers for public services and defense. For you, it raises the question of where your assistant lives and whose laws apply to it. Book 6 introduces sovereign AI and an assistant built in Europe.

### Speech recognition and text-to-speech

Speech recognition turns sound into text, and text-to-speech turns text back into the sound of a voice. Modern voices can pause, change pitch, and add emphasis. Dictation uses speech recognition so that you can speak instead of type, while a voice conversation adds spoken replies. Transcription tools use speech recognition to turn recordings into text, and they make mistakes with names, accents, and background noise. Chapter 1.6 explains both directions, and Chapter 3.7 covers voiceovers and transcription.

### Style card

A style card is a short description of a visual or writing style that you paste into prompts or save in a project. For images, it lists colors, the style of illustration or photography, the mood, and what to avoid. For decks, it adds fonts and the style of icons. A style card keeps a set of pieces consistent, because each prompt starts from the same instructions. Chapter 3.4 introduces it, and Chapter 3.10 reuses it.

### Sycophancy

Sycophancy is an AI's tendency to agree too easily or to praise your work. It happens because people tend to rate agreeable answers higher during human feedback training, and the model learns to tell people what they want to hear. Ask for the strongest objections, for a critique, or for the reasons you might be wrong. Treat a suspiciously smooth agreement as a reason to check. Chapter 1.8 explains why it happens.

## T

### Token

A token is a small piece of text, often part of a word, that an AI reads and writes. A model predicts one token at a time. Context windows, usage limits, and prices are often measured in tokens. A short, common word may be one token, and a long or unusual word may be several. You don't need to count tokens yourself. Knowing the idea explains why long chats and long documents have limits. Chapter 1.4 explains tokens.

### Training

Training is the process of learning from examples. The model starts with random weights, makes predictions, is told how close it came, and adjusts its weights, and it repeats this many times. Each pass through all the examples is called an epoch. Training happens before you use the assistant, and your chats don't normally change the model during a conversation. Chapters 1.2 and 1.3 let you watch training happen.

### Training cutoff

The training cutoff, also called the knowledge cutoff, is the date up to which a model's training data was collected. The model knows nothing about events after it unless the assistant searches the web. Models are often released months after their cutoff, so even a new assistant's built-in knowledge is already months old. For anything that changes, such as prices, laws, or product versions, turn on web search or ask "As of what date is this true?" Chapter 1.7 explains it.

### Trigger

A trigger is the event that starts an automation, such as a new email arriving, a form being submitted, or a set time of day. Every automation pairs a trigger with one or more actions. A well-chosen trigger runs only when you want it to, which keeps the automation from acting too often. Chapter 4.7 builds a trigger-and-action workflow.

## V

### Voice cloning

Voice cloning is the use of AI to copy a particular person's voice from a short recording. It has legitimate uses, such as restoring the voice of someone who has lost it, with their consent. Scammers use it to make a phone call sound like a relative or a manager. Agreeing a family code word is a simple protection, because a cloned voice doesn't know it. Chapter 3.7 introduces voice cloning, and Book 6 covers the scams.

## W

### Web search

Web search is an assistant feature that searches the internet, places the results in the context window, and writes an answer from them, usually with links to its sources. An assistant that isn't searching answers from training, which may be out of date, so turn web search on for anything recent or changeable. Search results are placed in the context for that chat only and don't change the model. Chapter 1.7 compares answers with search off and on, and Chapter 4.1 shows you how to research with it.
