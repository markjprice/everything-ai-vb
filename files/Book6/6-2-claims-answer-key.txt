CLAIM 1: Wright brothers, December 17, 1903, near Kitty Hawk
Verdict: CORRECT. Use.
The flights took place at Kill Devil Hills, about four miles south of the
town of Kitty Hawk, North Carolina. "Near Kitty Hawk" is accurate.
Good sources: the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum, which displays
the 1903 Wright Flyer, and the National Park Service page for the Wright
Brothers National Memorial.

CLAIM 2: Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius (212 degrees Fahrenheit) at sea level
Verdict: CORRECT. Use.
This holds at standard sea-level air pressure. At higher altitudes, water
boils at a lower temperature, so the "at sea level" part is important.
Good sources: a national weather service or a university chemistry or
physics department.

CLAIM 3: IRA limit of $6,000 a year, or $7,000 if you're 50 or older
Verdict: OUT OF DATE. Don't use as written.
$6,000 (plus a $1,000 catch-up contribution at 50 or older) was the limit
from 2019 to 2022. The limit has risen since: $7,000 in 2024 and 2025, and
$7,500 for 2026, with a $1,100 catch-up for people 50 and older.
Lesson: money figures, tax rules, and limits change, often every year. An
assistant can give a figure that was right when its training data was
collected. Check the current year on the primary source.
Good source: the IRS website, irs.gov (search "IRA contribution limits").

CLAIM 4: Mount Everest is 8,489 meters (27,851 feet) tall
Verdict: CHANGED NUMBER. Use with changes.
The height agreed by China and Nepal in 2020 is 8,848.86 meters (29,031.7
feet). The claim swapped two digits (8,489 instead of 8,849), and the feet
figure matches the wrong number, so the two units agree with each other
but both are wrong.
Lesson: a number that looks precise and is consistent across units can
still be wrong. "Does it make sense?" helps: many people know Everest is
"about 29,000 feet."
Good sources: National Geographic or an encyclopedia such as Britannica,
which report the 2020 survey.

CLAIM 5: Kennedy's inaugural address, "Let us never tire of building bridges..."
Verdict: INVENTED QUOTATION. Don't use.
This sentence doesn't appear in Kennedy's inaugural address of
January 20, 1961. The full text is published by the John F. Kennedy
Presidential Library and Museum (jfklibrary.org). Searching for the exact
words in quotation marks finds no original source. The address is famous
for a different line: "ask not what your country can do for you, ask what
you can do for your country."
Lesson: a quotation needs its original source. An invented quote often
sounds like the person, and is attached to a real, famous event.
